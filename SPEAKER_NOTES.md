# Speaker notes (target: 5 minutes)

## 1. The impossible wall: 25 sec
“Imagine throwing a ball at a wall. It bounces back. For an electron, however, there is a small chance it could appear on the other side. That is quantum tunneling.”

## 2. Classical intuition: 35 sec
Point to the energy line. “The ball’s energy is below the top of this hill, so classically it rolls back. There is no route through the hill.”

## 3. The quantum shift: 35 sec
“At atomic scales, particles are not simply miniature balls. We describe an electron with a wave of possibilities: a map of where it may be found.”

## 4. Tunneling: 50 sec
“When this wave reaches a barrier, it fades inside rather than ending abruptly. If the barrier is thin enough, a small part remains beyond it. That means there is a small probability of detecting the particle on the far side.”

## 5. What happens: 35 sec
Let the short Manim clip play once first. “Watch the large incoming wave meet the barrier, then notice the much smaller possibility that remains beyond it.”

“This is probabilistic. Repeat the experiment: most particles bounce back; a few appear beyond. We never observe half a particle. Each detection finds one whole particle.”

## 6. The odds: 30 sec
“A thin barrier is easier to cross than a thick one. The probability falls very quickly as a barrier gets thicker or higher.”

## 7. Evidence: 40 sec
“A scanning tunneling microscope uses this effect. A sharp tip sits extremely close to a surface. Electrons tunnel across the gap; tiny distance changes produce large current changes, allowing us to map individual atoms.”

## 8. Why it matters: 35 sec
“Tunneling helps fusion occur in stars, enables useful electronic devices, creates leakage problems as chips shrink, and powers atom-scale microscopes.”

## 9. Close: 25 sec
“Keep one idea: a quantum particle can cross a barrier it cannot cross classically, because it behaves like a wave of probability. Impossible in everyday physics; possible in quantum mechanics.”
